# Write For Smart Readers

Use this skill to produce prose that a serious reader can trust. Assume the reader is smart, busy, and willing to follow a real argument. Write what the reader needs to know to act, decide, or understand the subject. Make every sentence carry information. Cut anything that only fills space.

Define the outcome, reader, evidence, and constraint before drafting. Use those decisions to choose what belongs in the text and what can be cut.

## Success Criteria

The final text succeeds when:

- the reader can name the main claim after one pass
- the most important point appears before supporting detail
- each sentence adds a claim, evidence, constraint, transition, or necessary caveat
- the prose preserves the requested artifact, length, medium, and genre
- no new facts, examples, claims, citations, or metrics were invented
- the ending stops on the last useful point rather than a recap or slogan

## Core Read

Before writing, identify these five things. Do this silently unless the user asks for a plan.

- Real reader need: What does the reader need to know to act, decide, evaluate, or understand?
- Real outcome: What must the paragraph, slide, section, or post make the reader understand, decide, question, or remember?
- Real reader: Who needs to be convinced, informed, or oriented? Prefer the actual reader over a generic persona: professor, computational-linguistics peer, teammate, reviewer, recruiter, skeptical technical reader.
- Real evidence: What facts, examples, measurements, sources, code behavior, course concepts, or constraints support the claim?
- Real constraint: What pressure should shape the prose? Examples: 90 words, one slide, no recap ending, professor-facing precision, HTML card copy, no unsupported causal claim, no hidden test-set claim, no marketing voice.

If one of these is missing, write around the gap honestly or ask for the missing fact. Do not invent specificity.

## Instruction Discipline

Keep the instruction surface compact and explicit.

- Prefer outcome-first instruction over process-heavy instruction. Define what good looks like, what constraints matter, what evidence is available, and what the final answer should contain.
- Use hard words like "always", "never", "must", and "only" for true invariants: honesty, preservation, safety, source fidelity, and required output shape. For judgment calls, use decision rules.
- Preserve before improving. Keep the user's facts, medium, structure, length, citations, and intent before making the prose sharper.
- Stop when the output meets the user's requested artifact and the success criteria. Do not add an editing diary, meta-explanation, or extra options unless asked.

## Drafting Rules

Write from substance outward.

1. Start with the point, not the setup. Do not open by announcing the topic, praising the idea, or explaining why the field matters.
2. Front-load at every level. Put the conclusion before the detail in the document, section, paragraph, and sentence unless the genre requires suspense.
3. Keep one idea per sentence and one topic per paragraph. Split sentences that carry two claims, two actions, or a claim plus a separate caveat.
4. Use the strongest plain noun and verb available. Name the actor, object, action, evidence, and consequence when they matter.
5. Prefer concrete facts: the number, name, date, source, method, dataset, metric, object, or example. Cut vague abstractions such as "a range of", "going forward", and "in terms of" unless they are the exact technical term.
6. Let structure carry the argument. Avoid previewing and recapping unless the document is long enough to need it.
7. Prefer one precise example over a trio of generic examples.
8. Keep claims proportional to evidence. Academic and technical prose can hedge, but the hedge must protect accuracy rather than soften a weak sentence.
9. End on the real last useful point: a result, limitation, next decision, implication, or open question. Do not add an inspirational bow.

Default to plain, specific, restrained, evidence-led prose. Prefer credibility over persuasion. Name gaps and limits directly. Do not turn adjacent evidence into stronger proof than it supports.

## Medium Defaults

Reports and papers:
- Make the section's first paragraph answer the local research or evaluation question.
- Distinguish results, interpretation, and speculation.
- Keep dataset, split, metric, citation, and method language exact.
- Use hedges for uncertainty, not as social padding.

Slides:
- Put one claim on each slide.
- Make headings informative. "Why macro F1 matters here" beats "Results".
- Use fragments only when they are labels, not fake drama.
- Delete any sentence the speaker will say aloud but the slide does not need.

HTML purpose pages:
- Treat each block as a small argument or task surface, not a landing-page pitch.
- Explain what the reader can inspect, decide, or do.
- Avoid hero-copy slogans unless the page's job is actually promotional.

Articles, posts, and X.com drafts:
- Give the piece a position.
- Keep tension, doubt, irritation, or surprise only when it comes from the source material.
- Do not perform insight with one-line paragraphs, rhetorical questions, or reveal formatting.

## Structural Repairs

Treat these as failure modes. Do not replace them with synonyms; repair the sentence's job.

- Binary contrast: "not X but Y", "not just X", "this is not about X". Repair by stating the positive claim directly. If the contrast is real, explain the distinction with concrete terms.
- Claim-colon explanation: "The key point: X". Repair by writing an ordinary sentence that integrates the point and support.
- Empty importance: "This matters because", "the stakes are high", "the implication is significant". Repair by naming the actual stake, affected reader, or consequence.
- Fractal recap: intro previews, sections preview, sections recap, conclusion recaps. Repair by making each idea appear once.
- Buried conclusion: background or throat-clearing appears before the point. Repair by moving the conclusion, decision, or needed action first.
- Multi-idea sentence: one sentence carries two claims, a claim plus a separate caveat, or multiple actions. Repair by splitting it.
- Fake balance: "on one hand... on the other hand..." with no decision. Repair by taking the position the evidence supports, then naming the limit.
- Three-beat rhythm: three examples, three adjectives, three clipped sentences. Repair by using the number the thought needs.
- Stock closers: "Ultimately", "In conclusion", "This shows that". Repair by ending on the strongest remaining fact, caveat, or next action.
- Empty polish: smooth sentences that say nothing specific. Repair by adding an actor, object, method, number, source, consequence, or cut the sentence.

## Revision Pass

After drafting, run this pass before delivering.

- Information density: Can any sentence be removed without losing a claim, evidence, constraint, or useful transition? If yes, cut it.
- Front-loading: Does the document, section, paragraph, and sentence put the useful point before background? If not, reorder it.
- Unit discipline: Does each sentence carry one idea, and does each paragraph stay on one topic? If not, split or cut.
- Reader pressure: Would this satisfy a skeptical master's-level computational-linguistics peer or professor? If not, add precision or narrow the claim.
- Evidence boundary: Did any sentence overclaim, imply unsupported causality, blur train/dev/test distinctions, or turn adjacent experience into direct proof? Fix it.
- Rhythm: Do sentences and paragraphs vary naturally, or does everything sit at the same polite altitude? Vary only by clarifying the argument, not by adding decoration.
- Structure tells: Remove formulaic contrast, rhetorical self-questions, decorative fragments, generic recap, and mechanical bold-label bullets unless the medium requires them.

## Output

Match the user's requested artifact.

- For drafting, provide the finished prose first.
- For rewriting, provide the rewritten text first unless the user asked for a diagnosis.
- For review, lead with the highest-impact issues and quote only the necessary snippets.
- Keep notes short. The reader should see the improved writing, not a performance of editing.
